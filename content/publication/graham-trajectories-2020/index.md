## Why we asked

For a long time, the textbook view was that personality settles down early in adulthood and stays put. More recent work says traits do change, and that people change at different rates, but the studies disagree about how. Most of them also rely on a single dataset. If a study of retirees in Berlin and a study of veterans in Boston give different answers, it is hard to tell whether the people differ or the analyses do. We wanted to know how the Big Five traits change in midlife and older adulthood, and whether the answer holds up across many samples.

## What we did

We used a coordinated analysis. Before we looked for data, we wrote the analysis scripts. We then found 16 longitudinal studies, with more than 60,000 participants in all, that had measured personality at least three times, and we fit the same multilevel growth model in each one. Finally, we combined the estimates meta-analytically. In other words, we ran 16 close replications of one analysis rather than pooling everything into one dataset, which let us see how much the answers differed from sample to sample.

## What we found

On average, extraversion and openness declined (b = -0.09 per decade for each, on a 10-point scale), and conscientiousness declined a little less (b = -0.05). Neuroticism followed a U-shape: it fell through most of adulthood and rose again in later life. Agreeableness showed no consistent pattern (b = 0.02, 95% CI [-0.02, 0.07]). These are small changes, roughly a tenth of a point per decade, though they add up over a long life.

Most samples also showed that people differ in how much they change. The samples disagreed with one another quite a bit, and the measurement scale, the average age of the sample, and the country explained only part of that. Sex was associated with very little, apart from slightly steeper declines in neuroticism among women. Baseline age was associated with different trajectories for every trait. For conscientiousness, for example, declines were most evident among people who were over 60 when they started.

## What it means

The declines fit theories in which older adults drop the behaviors that work and family once demanded. We did not test that explanation, so it remains speculation. With only 16 samples, we also had limited power to explain why they differed.

Replicating an analysis across many datasets shows how much a finding depends on the sample.
